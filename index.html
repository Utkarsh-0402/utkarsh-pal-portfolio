LinkedIn AI Discovery — surfacing hiring posts the Jobs tab misses

Case 01

Recruiters often post openings straight to the feed — as text, images, or carousels — where LinkedIn's Jobs filters never see them.

Self-initiated Personal + peer validation MoSCoW prioritization No wireframes
Problem

Hiring posts shared informally on the feed don't appear in job search or recommendations, so active job seekers miss relevant opportunities despite filtering diligently.

My Role

Solo, self-initiated — problem framing, solution design, prioritization, and metrics, with no brief or team.

Outcome

A structural case study used to demonstrate product thinking — not built, shipped, or tested with real users.

Read the full case study

Context

I personally noticed I was missing hiring opportunities on LinkedIn that were shared as regular feed posts rather than proper Jobs listings, even while actively searching and filtering. Talking it through with 3–4 friends who were also job hunting confirmed it wasn't just me — they'd run into the same problem. This was informal peer validation, not a formal user research study.

Problem

  • Not getting all opportunities through the Jobs feature.
  • Difficulty applying multiple filters while searching for jobs.
  • Manually scanning the feed for hiring posts shared as images or carousels.
  • Hiring information in these posts is unstructured — hard to quickly identify role, location, experience, application link, or deadline.
  • Users get generic recommendations instead of ones personalized to their skills, interests, and career goals.

Process

Went straight to brainstorming solutions rather than first benchmarking competitors like Indeed or Naukri. Proposed four features: an AI Job Discovery tab aggregating AI-detected hiring content, a personalized feed using behavioral analytics, real-time notifications for high-match posts, and an AI match score against a user's profile.

Prioritized using MoSCoW, driven mainly by user impact — how many people would benefit — rather than technical effort:

  • Must have: AI Job Discovery tab (the MVP)
  • Should have: Personalized feed; notification on the Jobs icon
  • Could have: AI match score; AI resume suggestions
  • Won't have (this release): AI interview prep; AI learning recommendations

Defined six metrics: feed adoption rate, post click-through rate, application conversion rate, recommendation accuracy, time-to-discover, and CSAT/NPS.

Outcome

The case study itself is the deliverable — used to show product thinking to recruiters. It hasn't been shared externally for structured feedback yet.

Learning

Practiced translating a vague, personally-felt pain point into a structured problem statement, and got real practice weighing prioritization trade-offs using impact as the primary lens.

Honest gap: no competitor research or formal user interviews were conducted — a quick scan of how other job platforms handle unstructured hiring content would strengthen this further.

Instagram SafeConnect — making the first message feel less like a leap of faith

Case 02

Instagram makes it easy to discover people, but difficult to trust and confidently connect with strangers.

Self-initiated Secondary research Persona-based Structural / no wireframes
Problem

Fake accounts, privacy worries, fear of rejection, and social anxiety all discourage users from engaging with people they don't already know.

My Role

Solo, self-initiated — research synthesis, feature design, persona mapping, KPI definition, and roadmap.

Outcome

A portfolio case study demonstrating problem framing and feature design — not validated with real users.

Read the full case study

Context

This project came from a personal experience — running into a fake account or spam DM on Instagram — which made the trust and safety gap feel real rather than abstract.

Problem

Six sub-problems were identified through secondary research rather than primary interviews: fake accounts and bots (Facebook removed 1.3B fake accounts in 2020), privacy concerns (70%+ of users worry about data security, per Pew), fear of judgment or rejection, social anxiety linked to influencer browsing, fear of negative feedback (55% of users have faced trolling or harassment, per The Atlantic Council), and engagement-driven algorithms that deprioritize authentic interaction (per Harvard Business Review, 2021).

Process

Designed SafeConnect — a feature combining lightweight verification, shared-interest matching, privacy-controlled introduction requests, icebreaker prompts, AI-based safety filtering, and a re-weighted Explore feed. Mapped these features against two personas: Anjali (fears rejection/judgment) and Yash (social anxiety, privacy concerns). Defined KPIs across engagement, community-building, safety/trust, and satisfaction, and outlined a five-phase roadmap from research & validation through to continuous improvement — the first phase of which proposes real user interviews as a next step, not something already completed.

Outcome

The case study itself is the deliverable, used to demonstrate product thinking. It hasn't been built, shipped, or tested with real users.

Learning

Practiced breaking a personally-felt problem into named, research-backed sub-problems, then translating that into a feature set with personas and metrics attached.

Honest gap: no formal prioritization (like impact-vs-effort scoring) was applied across the seven proposed features, and no wireframes were built — worth adding if this case study is revisited.

Voice AI — mapping the market before proposing a product

Case 03

Before designing anything, I wanted to understand where Voice AI's biggest opportunities and constraints actually are.

Market research Desk study Not a shipped product
Focus

Market sizing, competitive landscape, and adoption challenges across the Voice AI ecosystem.

Method

Secondary research compiled into a structured market study — not a product case study.

Output

A report identifying opportunity areas: multilingual, privacy-focused, and industry-specific Voice AI.

Read the full research summary

Why this is labeled "Market Research," not a case study

Unlike the other two entries, this project has no user problem I identified, no product I designed, and no MVP or prioritization decisions — it's an industry study. It's included here because it shows research and analytical range, but it's framed honestly as its own format rather than forced into a case-study shape.

Key findings

  • The Voice AI market is projected to grow from $20.25B (2023) to $50B (2030) at a 22.3% CAGR.
  • Consumer use spans smart assistants, smart homes, and voice commerce (projected at $164B by 2025); enterprise use spans customer support, healthcare, banking, and retail.
  • Core challenges: transcription accuracy (85–95%, varying with noise), privacy concerns (79% of consumers worry about surveillance), and high development costs ($500K–$5M).
  • Market leaders: Amazon Alexa (69% smart speaker share), Google Assistant (25%), plus Apple Siri, Microsoft Cortana, IBM Watson, and SoundHound.
  • Opportunity areas: industry-specific Voice AI (healthcare CAGR of 26.7%), multilingual/accent-adaptive AI, and privacy-focused on-device processing (growing 28% YoY).

AI Text-to-Image Evaluation for India — is "visually good" the same as "advertisement-ready"?

Case 04

A human-centered evaluation of three text-to-image models, tested against a real advertising use case for an Indian fashion brand.

Human evaluation 9 participants Cross-model comparison Quant + qualitative
Problem

Generic image-generation benchmarks measure visual quality, but not whether an output is actually usable for a specific market like India.

My Role

Owned the evaluation end-to-end — use case, prompt design, human evaluation design, data collection, analysis, and product implications.

Outcome

A clear preference result (Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite chosen by 7/9 participants) plus a product-level insight about what "quality" means for this market.

Read the full case study

Overview

I designed and ran a human-centered evaluation of three text-to-image models — Google Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite Image, Ideogram 3.0, and FLUX.1 Schnell — to understand how well each could generate advertising creatives for an Indian fashion brand. Rather than judging image quality in isolation, I tested all three against one real use case: a festive digital advertisement for an Indian ethnic clothing brand.

Problem

Generic text-to-image benchmarks focus on image quality, realism, or technical capability — but those metrics don't capture whether an output is actually useful for a specific market. For Indian fashion brands, an advertisement needs to follow the creative brief while accurately representing Indian people, lifestyle, cultural context, and marketing information. The question this evaluation set out to answer: can AI-generated images move beyond "visually good" to become usable advertising creatives for Indian audiences?

Why It Matters for India

India is a highly diverse market — festivals, clothing, aesthetics, languages, and consumer preferences vary widely by region. A generic globally-trained image can look visually polished while still feeling inauthentic to an Indian audience. AI-generated advertising only delivers real value to businesses if the output is actually usable for the local market, not just technically impressive.

Methodology

The same prompt was used across all three models — a premium festive campaign image for a fictional brand, "Sanskriti Fashion," specifying an Indian woman in a maroon embroidered kurta set, a Diwali-decorated setting with diyas and rangoli, a 10% festive discount callout, and commercial photography quality. Outputs were anonymized as Image A, B, and C so participants couldn't be influenced by which model made which image.

9 participants rated each image independently on a 5-point scale across 7 criteria: prompt adherence, image realism, Indian representation, advertisement readiness, background realism, Indian lifestyle reflection, and text/spelling accuracy — then picked one overall preference.

Results

  • Image A — Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite: chosen by 7/9 participants (77.8%)
  • Image B — Ideogram 3.0: chosen by 2/9 participants (22.2%)
  • Image C — FLUX.1 Schnell: chosen by 0/9 participants (0%)

Image A scored 4.44/5 on realism, but scored even higher — 4.67/5 — on advertisement readiness. I'd initially expected the most realistic image to win outright; instead, participant feedback showed that premium aesthetics, visual appeal, and commercial usefulness mattered just as much as realism itself.

Key Takeaway

AI evaluation is a product problem, not just a model-quality problem. This project taught me to evaluate AI outputs against the actual user job-to-be-done, combine quantitative ratings with human judgment, and translate those findings into product decisions. For Indian users, building useful AI products also means evaluating local context, cultural relevance, and real-world usability — not just generic technical quality.
